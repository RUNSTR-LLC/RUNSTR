# RUNSTR Overview

> Product overview, business model, and technical narrative. Written for investors, partners, press, and internal alignment.

---

## Project Overview

RUNSTR is an anonymous fitness tracking app that rewards users with real money for staying active. It lets users track cardio workouts — running, walking, and cycling — via GPS, then pays them micro rewards (50 sats per daily workout) delivered directly to their wallet. The app is built with React Native and Expo, uses a decentralized identity protocol for authentication and social sharing, and a cloud backend for competitions and reward payments. Users log in with a private key, and their profile is imported automatically from the network — no email, no password, no personal data collected.

The user flow is streamlined into a three-tab interface: Profile, Teams, and Rewards. From the Profile tab, users start GPS-tracked workouts through a swipe-based activity grid, view their history, and join competitions. The Teams tab presents 17 charities — selecting a "team" means choosing a charity to support. Users control where their rewards go: they can keep them, route them as micro donations to their selected charity, or top up their PPQ.AI credit balance to chat with an LLM. The Rewards tab shows total earnings, donation history, and a transparency dashboard that exposes the global reward pool and payout breakdown. Every cardio workout with distance greater than zero is automatically submitted to the backend, where an external service validates it, sends the reward, and records the payment.

The architecture follows a local-first, cache-first philosophy. Workouts are saved to local storage immediately, then submitted to the backend in the background. A single global connection instance manages all relay connections, preventing the connection explosion that would come from multiple services each opening their own WebSocket connections. State is managed through a combination of React Context, Zustand stores, and TTL-based in-memory caches. Workout events are structured locally for signing and tag generation, then submitted to the database — which serves as the single source of truth for competition data and reward tracking.

RUNSTR extends well past basic fitness tracking. Users can earn PPQ.AI credits through their workouts and use them to chat with an LLM that has full context of their health and fitness data — workouts, habits, journal entries, mood, steps, and more. Through the OpenClaw integration, users can also give any external AI agent access to that same data, opening the door to personalized insights from any tool in the ecosystem. The encrypted backup system lets users export and restore their entire workout history, habits, journal entries, and preferences across devices, fully encrypted so only the user can access their own data.

Competitions are managed through the backend with hardcoded event definitions like Season II, the January Walking Contest, and Running Bitcoin. When users join a competition, their auto-submitted workouts count toward leaderboards ranked by distance, duration, or workout count. Anti-cheat measures include pace limits that reject impossibly fast workouts, distance caps per session, split consistency checks that flag uneven pacing, and car detection for speed anomalies. Flagged workouts are stored but excluded from leaderboard rankings. The reward system uses atomic flags to prevent duplicate daily claims, and a polling service notifies users via toast when their payment has been confirmed. Rewards flow to one of three destinations — the user's wallet, their chosen charity as a micro donation, or their PPQ.AI credit balance — giving every workout a tangible, meaningful outcome.

## AI Implementation

RUNSTR turns every workout into fuel for AI. When users select PPQ.AI as their team, their daily micro rewards — 50 sats per cardio workout — are converted into LLM credits instead of being sent to a wallet or charity. Account creation is one tap: the app generates PPQ.AI credentials stored locally on the device, and users can top up with preset amounts via Lightning invoices whenever they want more. The result is a self-sustaining loop where physical effort earns access to intelligence — run more, chat more. Credits cost fractions of a cent per query, so even a single workout funds dozens of conversations.

The AI Health Dashboard gives users a chat interface backed by their complete health and fitness context. When a user sends a message, the RunstrContextGenerator assembles a rich snapshot: the last 30 days of workouts with pace, distance, and calories; the last 7 days of journal entries with mood and energy scores; current habit streaks and check-in status; body composition estimates like BMI and VO2 Max; and even recent diet and nutrition logs. All of this is injected as system context into the LLM call, so the AI isn't giving generic advice — it's responding to what you've actually been doing. Users can choose from a range of models including Claude Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and others, switching freely from the dashboard header.

Beyond workouts, RUNSTR captures the qualitative side of health. The journal lets users write daily entries tagged with a five-level mood scale and a 1-5 energy rating, with streak tracking to encourage consistency. The habit tracker supports both abstinence habits (quit smoking, no sugar) and positive habits (daily meditation, reading), each with automatic streak calculation and check-in history. All of this data feeds directly into the AI context window. When a user asks "how am I doing this week?" the AI can correlate a dip in mood with skipped workouts, notice that energy ratings climb on days with morning runs, or flag a broken meditation streak before it compounds.

The RUNSTR Fitness skill extends data access beyond the app's own chat window. Through the encrypted backup system, users can hand their credentials to any compatible AI agent — whether it's Claude Code, OpenClaw, or any platform supporting the AgentSkills standard — and that agent instantly has the full picture: every run, walk, and cycling session, habit streaks, journal reflections, mood trends, and step counts. The skill queries open relays, decrypts the user's backup on their local machine, and parses the workout history. And the PPQ.AI credits earned through workouts aren't locked inside RUNSTR either — users can take their credentials to the PPQ.AI website for access to 500+ models spanning image generation, music creation, video chat, deep research, and more. A morning run doesn't just earn a chat with a health-focused LLM; it earns access to an entire AI platform.

The entire AI stack is built around a simple principle: your effort earns your access. Encrypted backups published to open relays can only be decrypted by the user's own keys — journal entries, mood, habits, and workout history are locked to the user and no one else. AI chat conversations never leave the device at all. PPQ.AI credentials are stored locally and fully portable — users can plug them into the PPQ.AI website, other apps, or any agent that supports the API. There's no subscription, no monthly fee — just workouts converted into credits at the pace the user sets. The AI isn't a premium upsell bolted onto a fitness app; it's a natural extension of the reward system. You track your health, you earn the right to interrogate it, and you choose exactly which agents get to see it.

## Charity & Donations

Every workout in RUNSTR is an opportunity to support a cause. When users select a charity as their team, their daily micro rewards — earned just by completing a cardio workout — are routed directly to that organization as a micro donation. Users don't have to think about it: they run, walk, or cycle, and their chosen charity receives funding automatically. It's a passive giving model where the act of staying healthy generates real financial support for the causes users care about, one workout at a time.

The app ships with 17 hardcoded charities, projects, and community groups that users can support. The roster spans continents and missions: the ALS Network funds research and patient care, the Human Rights Foundation defends civil liberties worldwide, and grassroots circular economy projects like Bitcoin Ekasi (South Africa), Afribit Kibera (Kenya), Bitcoin Isla (Mexico), Bitcoin House Bali (Indonesia), and Bitcoin Basin (New Zealand) are building local economic infrastructure in underserved communities. Closer to home, Bitcoin Veterans supports service members, Bitcoin District builds community in Washington DC, and Central Pennsylvania Bitcoiners organizes locally. Developer-focused projects like Ashigaru, BuhoGO, and WeSatoshi round out the list. Users can also select RUNSTR itself or PPQ.AI as their team, directing rewards toward the app's development or their own AI credit balance. Every charity has a direct payment address, so users can also donate outside the app from any compatible wallet.

RUNSTR runs competitions that turn individual workouts into collective fundraising efforts. Season II is a two-month distance-based competition (January through March 2026) with a prize pool split between top performers and charity payouts — participants pay an entry fee, and the combined pool is distributed across running, walking, and cycling leaderboards, with a portion going to the charities those participants support. The Running Bitcoin Challenge honors Hal Finney by challenging participants to cover 21 km of running and walking within a three-week window, with all finishers earning a reward and donations flowing to the ALS Network. These in-app events give users something to compete for while amplifying the charitable impact of every kilometer logged.

Beyond digital competitions, RUNSTR is launching in-person race events starting with an inaugural 5K at Haines Point in Washington DC on March 15, 2026, produced in partnership with Bitcoin District and Pubkey DC. The event combines a professional race experience — permits, timing chips, finish line, post-race brunch — with a charity component where the most-represented charity among participants receives an additional prize pool donation. Virtual participation lets users anywhere in the world run their own 5K during the event window and appear on the same leaderboard as in-person runners. The DC event is the proof of concept for scaling to Texas, Tennessee, Mexico, and eventually over a hundred events per year, each one generating revenue that flows back into the rewards pool, charity donations, and app development.

The charity model creates a flywheel: users work out, workouts generate micro donations, donations attract more charities to the platform, more charities attract more users who care about those causes, and more users mean larger prize pools and more impactful events. The transparency dashboard on the Rewards tab shows exactly where the money goes — global reward pool balances, charity payout leaderboards, and pending distributions — so users can see the real-world impact of their effort. It's not a pledge or a promise; it's verifiable micro donations generated by putting one foot in front of the other.

## Rewards

RUNSTR pays users for working out. Complete a cardio workout — running, walking, or cycling — and earn 50 sats, delivered straight to whatever Lightning address you plug into the app's Settings. There's no points system, no in-app currency that expires, no gift cards with restrictions. It's real value, sent to a real wallet, every single day you show up. The reward is capped at one per day to keep the system sustainable, but the bar is simple: track a workout from any eligible source — GPS, manual entry, Apple Health, or Health Connect — and the reward is yours.

Where your rewards go is entirely up to you. Enter a Lightning address and your 50 sats land in your wallet. Select a charity as your team and skip the Lightning address, and your rewards flow automatically as micro donations to that organization. Or choose PPQ.AI as your team and convert your workout rewards into AI credits. The routing is implicit and frictionless — the presence or absence of a Lightning address determines the path, with your selected charity always serving as the fallback. Every workout generates value no matter what; the only question is where it goes.

Beyond daily rewards, RUNSTR runs events that raise the earning potential significantly. Season II is a two-month competition (January through March 2026) with a 1 million sat prize pool — 500,000 sats distributed to top performers across running, walking, and cycling leaderboards, and another 500,000 sats split across the charities represented by participants. The Running Bitcoin Challenge honors Hal Finney by awarding 1,000 sats to every finisher who covers 21 km of running and walking, with donations directed to the ALS Network. Einundzwanzig participants earn double rewards — 100 sats per workout instead of the standard 50. And the upcoming RUNSTR 5K in Washington DC on March 15 brings competition offline, with in-person and virtual participants sharing the same leaderboard and charity prize pool.

The Rewards tab doesn't just show what you've earned — it shows where the money comes from and where it goes. The Transparency Dashboard displays the global rewards pool balance, total sats paid out, charity payout leaderboards ranked by donation volume, and pending batch payments for organizations that accumulate before payout. Every confirmed payment is recorded with proof, and a polling service notifies users via toast the moment their reward lands. Users can see their personal breakdown — how much went to their wallet, how much went to charity, and what's still pending — giving full visibility into the system without having to trust a black box.

The reward system is designed to scale. In-person event revenue, Season II entry fees, and sponsorships all feed back into the rewards pool, meaning the more the community grows, the more there is to distribute. The model doesn't rely on outside funding or token speculation — it's built on a straightforward exchange: you put in the miles, you get paid, and a portion goes to causes that matter. Whether you're a competitive runner chasing a Season II leaderboard spot, a casual walker earning daily rewards for your charity, or someone stacking sats one workout at a time, the system works the same way for everyone.

## Workouts & Privacy

RUNSTR doesn't ask for your name, your email, your phone number, or any personal information. There's no account creation, no KYC, no sign-up form. Users authenticate with a cryptographic key pair from a decentralized protocol — a private key that stays on their device and a public key that serves as their identity. That's it. No centralized user database, no profile tied to a corporate server, no data broker has anything to sell. You can use the app without ever revealing who you are, and the system works exactly the same whether you're anonymous or not.

The app tracks cardio workouts via GPS with real-time metrics — distance, pace, elevation, split times, calories — across running, walking, cycling, and hiking. Beyond workouts, users can journal daily with mood ratings and energy levels, build habits with streak tracking, and log diet and nutrition. All of this personal, reflective data is stored locally on the device and nowhere else. Your journal entries, mood trends, habit check-ins, and AI chat conversations never leave your phone unless you explicitly choose to back them up. There's no cloud sync running in the background, no analytics pipeline harvesting your habits, no third party watching your mood fluctuate.

When users want to preserve their data — or move it to a new device — they tap Backup in Settings. The app collects everything (workouts, steps, habits, journal entries, preferences), compresses it, and encrypts it with the user's own keys using NIP-44 self-encryption. The encrypted payload is published to decentralized relays where it can be retrieved from any device, but only the user can decrypt it. No one operating those relays can read the contents. No one at RUNSTR can read the contents. The backup is locked to the user's private key, period. Restore works the same way in reverse — fetch from relays, decrypt locally, and all your data is back.

RUNSTR is free. There's no subscription tier, no premium unlock, no feature gating behind a monthly fee. GPS tracking, workout history, journal, habits, mood tracking, encrypted backup, charity donations, and daily rewards are all available to every user from day one. The app is open source, and the reward system pays users rather than charging them. Some competitions and in-person events may require a ticket or entry fee, which funds prize pools and charity donations — but the core experience of tracking your fitness privately and earning rewards for it costs nothing.

Because RUNSTR uses a decentralized protocol for identity instead of a traditional account system, users own their keys and their data outright. There's no "forgot password" flow because there's no password — there's a private key that the user controls. There's no "delete my account" request to a support team because there's no account on a server to delete. If you stop using the app, your encrypted backup sits on decentralized relays until you decide to retrieve it, and your local data disappears when you clear the app. No residual profile lingers on a corporate database. The tradeoff is real — lose your key and there's no recovery email — but the benefit is equally real: no one can lock you out, suspend your account, or monetize your data without your knowledge.

## Business Model

RUNSTR's business model is straightforward: sell tickets to fitness events. No venture capital, no token sales, no subscription fees, no in-app advertising. Revenue comes from two sources — ticket sales and event sponsorships. The plan is to run paid virtual events monthly and larger in-person events quarterly, each one generating revenue while doubling as a brand-building opportunity. In-person events attract sponsorships from local businesses and aligned brands, while virtual events scale globally with minimal overhead. The goal is profitability through a repeating calendar of events that people actually want to participate in, not through extracting value from users or selling their data.

RUNSTR's target audience is wider than it looks. Fitness enthusiasts get in-person and virtual race events to compete in. Bitcoiners get real rewards for their workouts. Nostr users get a native fitness tracker built on the protocol they already use. AI enthusiasts get an agent-interoperable fitness app that lets them plug their health data into any compatible agent. The app serves all four without adding complexity — the same simple three-tab interface handles rewards, charity donations, competitions, encrypted backups, and AI credits. There's no mode-switching or feature bloat. A user who only cares about earning rewards and a user who only cares about giving their agent fitness context both get what they want from the same product.

The rewards pool is effectively RUNSTR's marketing budget — except users decide where it goes. Every workout generates a micro donation to whatever charity the user supports, which means every charity listed in the app has a direct financial incentive to send their community to RUNSTR. It behooves every organization, project, and team in the app to engage their user base and let their community know they can generate donations just by working out. This creates a natural partnership model: RUNSTR doesn't have to cold-call charities for promotion — the charities promote RUNSTR because it's a revenue stream for their cause. The more users a charity drives to the app, the more donations flow their way. Both sides win without a traditional marketing spend.

Each event — virtual or in-person — is a marketing opportunity that compounds. In-person events generate race-day footage, finish-line celebrations, charity announcement moments, sponsor highlights, and post-race content that gets cut into social media shorts, recaps, and promotional material. Virtual events generate leaderboard drama, global participation stories, and community engagement across time zones. The content feeds future event promotion, which drives more ticket sales, which funds more events. Sponsorship opportunities at in-person events bring in additional revenue while giving brands visibility through broadcast, social media, and on-course placement. The quarterly in-person events are the tentpole moments; the monthly virtual events keep the community engaged and the revenue flowing between them.

RUNSTR sits at an intersection that makes people want to root for it. It's open source. It pays users instead of charging them. It funnels money to charities. It respects privacy. It doesn't lock data behind proprietary APIs. A user who cares about any combination of fitness events, rewards, charity, open-source software, decentralized identity, or agent interoperability has a reason to buy a ticket, join an event, and tell their friends. The bet is simple: build something people genuinely believe in, run events they genuinely want to attend, and let the community do the marketing that no ad budget could buy.

## Events & Competitions

RUNSTR events are built around the race formats people already know and train for — 5Ks, 10Ks, half marathons, and marathons. Both running and walking count. Whether you're a competitive runner chasing a personal record or someone who walks their first 5K, there's an event for you. Virtual events run monthly, letting users anywhere in the world compete on the same leaderboard by tracking their workout in the app during the event window. Quarterly in-person events bring the community together at real courses with timing, finish lines, and post-race gatherings. The format is familiar; the reward structure is what makes it different.

When a user joins a competition, they don't have to opt in per workout or remember to submit anything. Every qualifying cardio workout tracked via GPS in the app is automatically submitted to the competition leaderboard. Workouts imported from Apple Health or Health Connect sync when the user opens the app, and those count too. Run on Monday, walk on Wednesday, cycle on Friday — all of it counts toward your total distance for the event. Leaderboards are split by activity type (running, walking, cycling), so users compete within their discipline. Results and standings will be visible on the RUNSTR website alongside event sign-ups, making it easy to check rankings and discover upcoming competitions without opening the app.

Open competitions invite gaming, so RUNSTR validates every submission. Pace limits reject impossibly fast workouts. Distance caps flag sessions that exceed what's physically reasonable. Split consistency checks look for uneven pacing that suggests a car ride with a brief jog at the end. Speed anomaly detection catches workouts that look more like driving than running. Flagged workouts are stored but excluded from leaderboards, so honest participants aren't competing against fabricated numbers. The system isn't perfect, but it's aggressive enough to keep the leaderboards credible and the prize pools fair.

Events offer multiple ways to earn. Season II distributes 500,000 sats to top performers across running, walking, and cycling leaderboards, and another 500,000 sats to the charities represented by those participants — meaning your distance doesn't just earn you a spot on the board, it earns money for the cause you support. The Running Bitcoin Challenge awards 1,000 sats to every finisher who hits the 21 km goal. Some events offer boosted daily rewards — Einundzwanzig participants earn 100 sats per workout instead of the standard 50. Users who route rewards to PPQ.AI earn AI credits instead of sats, so event prizes can fund their agent usage. The structure varies by event, but the principle is consistent: compete, and something of value flows to you, your charity, or both.

The vision is a steady drumbeat of events — monthly virtual competitions that anyone can join from anywhere, and quarterly in-person races that anchor the community calendar. Each in-person event is a chance to partner with local organizations, attract sponsorships, generate race-day content for social media, and introduce new users to the app. Each virtual event keeps the community engaged between tentpole moments, with leaderboard stakes and charity impact that give every workout during the event window extra weight. As the event calendar grows, so does the content library, the sponsorship pipeline, and the community itself — every race is both a standalone experience and fuel for the next one.

## Brainstorm: Infrastructure & Data Distribution

The RUNSTR website needs to become more than a landing page — it should be the public face of every competition. Event details, leaderboard standings, sign-up links, and results should all be accessible on the web without requiring the app. The website reads directly from Supabase, which is the source of truth for all competition and leaderboard data. This gives the website real-time accuracy — when a user finishes a workout and refreshes the leaderboard page, their result is there immediately, no delay. Since the website is RUNSTR's own infrastructure, giving it Supabase read access is straightforward and secure.

The RUNSTR Fitness skill needs a different path. The skill runs on the user's machine — it can't have Supabase credentials, and it shouldn't. But it already knows how to query Nostr relays, because that's how it fetches encrypted workout backups. So the solution is to publish competition data to Nostr, where the skill can pick it up using the same open protocol it already uses. When a user asks their agent "how am I doing in Season II?" or "what competitions are open right now?", the skill queries Nostr relays for structured competition events — no API key, no backend dependency, no special access required. Without this, the skill is blind to the competitive side of the app.

The RUNSTR Zapper — a locally hosted tool that manages reward payments — is the bridge. It already has read/write access to Supabase, so adding a cron job that reads leaderboard state every 10 minutes and publishes it to Nostr as replaceable events is a natural extension. The Nostr events would use standardized tags — something like `["d", "season2-running-leaderboard"]` — so each publish overwrites the last, and the skill always gets a current snapshot. The Zapper is already touching every workout as it processes rewards; formatting that data into Nostr events and publishing it is lightweight additional work.

The skill's value grows significantly once it can answer competition questions. Right now it gives agents access to workout history, habits, journal entries, and mood — the personal fitness picture. Adding competition awareness means the agent can also tell you where you stand on a leaderboard, how many kilometers you need to catch the person above you, when the next event opens for registration, and what the prize structure looks like. It transforms the skill from a personal fitness reader into a full competition companion. Users who interact with their agent between workouts get a reason to open the app and put in another session.

The architecture is clean: Supabase is the single source of truth. The website and app read from Supabase directly for real-time data. The Zapper reads from Supabase every 10 minutes and publishes snapshots to Nostr. The RUNSTR skill reads from Nostr for decentralized, credential-free access. Data flows one direction — Supabase to Nostr — with no circular dependencies. Three consumers, two read paths, one cron job bridging them. The result is an ecosystem where competition data reaches every surface that needs it — app, website, and agent — each reading from whichever source makes sense for its context.

## Pain Points & Known Issues

Step counting on GrapheneOS has been a persistent headache. GrapheneOS has a custom "Sensors" permission that, when disabled, doesn't throw an error — it silently returns zeroed sensor data. Users see "0 steps" and assume the app is broken, when in reality the operating system is blocking sensor access by design. RUNSTR has built a PrivacyROMDetectionService that identifies GrapheneOS, CalyxOS, LineageOS, and other privacy-focused ROMs and attempts to surface helpful guidance, but the fundamental issue — that the OS lies about sensor availability instead of denying permission — makes it difficult to diagnose automatically. Users on these devices need to manually enable Sensors in their app permissions, and that's not obvious.

Leaderboard accuracy has been a recurring problem. Users have reported seeing incorrect rankings, stale data, or workouts that don't appear on the board despite being tracked successfully. The root causes vary: cache TTLs that hold onto outdated snapshots too long, race conditions between workout submission and leaderboard refresh, and edge cases where workouts are submitted but haven't been validated by the anti-cheat filters yet. When a user finishes a run and immediately checks the leaderboard, they expect to see their workout reflected — any delay erodes trust in the competition system. Tighter cache invalidation and real-time leaderboard updates are needed to close this gap.

Reward reliability has been the most trust-damaging issue. Users complete a workout, see the local confirmation that they've earned a reward, and then wait — sometimes indefinitely — for the actual payment to arrive. The external reward service that monitors Supabase and sends Lightning payments has experienced downtime, routing failures to certain Lightning address providers, and silent failures where payments simply don't process. Charity addresses hosted on BTCPay Server and Geyser Fund nodes have been particularly problematic due to Lightning routing limitations from the payment wallet. When rewards feel unpredictable, users stop believing the core promise of the app: work out, get paid.

The GPS tracker works well for most users but fails for a meaningful minority. Reports include distance freezing after the first few seconds, phantom distance accumulating during GPS signal loss (tunnels, dense buildings), and the tracker not starting at all on certain Android devices. The underlying issues range from stale location subscriptions that don't clean up properly, to accuracy thresholds that are too aggressive or too lenient depending on the environment, to device-specific quirks in how expo-location handles background tracking. Every GPS failure is a lost workout — and potentially a lost reward — so these bugs hit harder than typical UI issues.

Health Connect integration on Android has proven fragile. Some users report that workouts tracked in other fitness apps (Samsung Health, Google Fit) don't sync into RUNSTR, or sync with incorrect data. The issues stem from Health Connect's permission model (which requires explicit per-data-type grants that users often skip), version incompatibilities across Android versions, and timing issues where the app queries Health Connect before the source app has finished writing its data. Unlike HealthKit on iOS — which has a mature, well-documented API — Health Connect is still evolving, and its behavior varies across device manufacturers. Users who rely on imported workouts for their daily reward are the most affected, since a failed sync means a missed reward with no obvious workaround.
